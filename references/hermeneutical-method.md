# Historical-Grammatical Method Reference

This document defines the exegetical method the Bible Exegesis Agent follows.

## Definition

The historical-grammatical method seeks to determine the meaning of a biblical
text by analyzing:

1. **The grammar and syntax** of the original language
2. **The historical context** in which the text was written
3. **The literary context** — the passage's role within its immediate and broader
   literary setting

The goal is to recover **authorial intent** — what the original author meant
to communicate to the original audience.

## Steps of exegesis

### Step 1: Establish the text
- Determine the best reading (textual criticism if needed)
- Identify the original language text
- Select appropriate translations for comparison

### Step 2: Observe the text
- Read the passage multiple times
- Note repeated words, key terms, structural markers
- Identify genre and literary form
- Map the structure (outline, chiasm, parallelism)

### Step 3: Determine the historical context
- Author: Who wrote this? When? Where? Why?
- Audience: Who were the original recipients?
- Occasion: What prompted this text?
- Setting: What was the political, social, religious environment?

### Step 4: Analyze the grammar
- Parse key verbs (tense, voice, mood/aspect)
- Identify clause relationships
- Note word order (especially departures from normal order)
- Analyze particles, conjunctions, and discourse markers

### Step 5: Perform lexical analysis
- Identify key terms
- Determine semantic range
- Establish meaning from context (not etymology)
- Survey usage in the same author, same corpus, broader canon

### Step 6: Consider the literary context
- Immediate context (surrounding verses and paragraphs)
- Book-level context (the argument or narrative of the whole book)
- Canonical context (how this text relates to the rest of Scripture)

### Step 7: Synthesize
- State the meaning of the text based on the evidence gathered
- Note confidence level
- Identify areas of genuine ambiguity
- Distinguish the text's meaning from its possible theological implications

## What this method is NOT

- **Not allegory** — does not look for hidden spiritual meanings behind the text
- **Not reader-response** — meaning resides in the text, not in the reader
- **Not proof-texting** — does not extract verses from context to support
  pre-existing conclusions
- **Not theological systematizing** — does not force texts into a systematic
  framework; it lets each text speak on its own terms first

## Relationship to other methods

The historical-grammatical method can be **supplemented** by:
- Discourse analysis (for mapping argument structure)
- Rhetorical criticism (for analyzing persuasive strategy)
- Narrative criticism (for analyzing story technique)
- Intertextual analysis (for tracing cross-references and allusions)

It should be **distinguished** from:
- Historical-critical method (which may question the text's historicity or unity)
- Allegorical method (which looks for meanings beyond the literal)
- Canonical criticism (which prioritizes the final form over the original context)

The agent uses the historical-grammatical method as its foundation and draws on
supplementary methods as analytical tools, not as replacements.
